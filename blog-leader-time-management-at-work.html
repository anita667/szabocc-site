Leader time management at work: remove before you add

You want your leaders to lead.

To coach. To decide. To explain the change to their teams.

Look at their calendars. They are busy doing the work.

That is not a motivation problem. It is a time problem. Leader time management at work comes before any training.

Leader time management at work starts with the load

DDI asked European leaders whether they have enough time for their tasks. Only 19% said yes (DDI Europe Leadership Report 2025).

Four in five do not.

The load is not only about hours. A Gartner survey of more than 6,000 employees and managers found that managers carry 51% more responsibility than they can handle effectively. 35% have more direct reports. 49% have more complex tasks (Gartner data, via HC Magazine, 2023).

Gartner's Jonathan Tabah put the consequence in one line: "you can't train your way out of an unmanageable set of responsibilities."

So the usual answer fails. A programme on top of a full calendar adds a task. It does not add time.

What a leader does when time runs out

Leadership is the work that can wait. The report is due today. The coaching conversation is not.

So the leader:

  • takes the task back instead of delegating it
  • skips the one-to-one that was moved twice already
  • decides fast, alone, because explaining takes longer
  • reacts all day and thinks never

Delegation is the clearest example. Delegating takes time first, and pays back later. Without time, it never starts.

DDI's 2025 research ties the sense of having time directly to burnout. Where leaders have room to think, worry about burnout falls (DDI Global Leadership Forecast 2025, via HC Magazine).

Why this lands on your desk

Nobody chooses a full calendar on purpose. It fills one request at a time.

A new priority arrives. A reporting line is added. A change programme starts, and the leaders who must carry it keep their old targets.

Each request is reasonable. Together they leave no room to lead.

The people who set the load sit above the leaders who carry it. That is why the fix starts with you, not with them.

Ask one question in your next leadership meeting: what did we add this year, and what did we take away?

If the answer is only the first half, you have found the problem.

Five steps for this month

You do not need a new programme. You need to take things off the table.

1. Count where the hours go. Ask each leader to list, for one week, what filled their calendar. Three columns: work only they can do, work they could hand over, work that should not exist. Watch the third column.

2. Remove before you add. For every leader, take one recurring task, report or meeting off their plate. Do it before you offer any development.

3. Hand over one task on purpose. Pick one task each leader still does that someone on the team could own. Name the person. Set the date. Agree what "good" looks like.

4. Protect one hour. One block a week, in the calendar, for the work that has no deadline: the team conversation, the decision that keeps coming back. Defend it like a customer meeting.

5. Build the learning into the work. Development that needs extra evenings will lose to the inbox. Use the real week: the next team meeting, the next hard conversation, the next decision.

What the calendar cannot show

A calendar shows where time goes. It does not show how the leader is experienced.

The GLA360 adds that view. Between 5 and 15 colleagues, chosen by the leader across boss, peers and team, rate the leader on 15 observable competencies. The results are compared with 19,000 leaders worldwide.

It shows whether the leader is seen as someone who empowers, develops and decides, or as someone who does it all. What comes out is a short plan: two Leadership Growth Areas and the steps behind them.

Coaching makes the plan a habit, inside the working week. No extra course. The leader works on a real situation, with a real deadline.

Start with one calendar

If your leaders are stuck in the work, find out where the time goes before you decide what to add.
