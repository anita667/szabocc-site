Leading through change: it is decided at the top and succeeds or fails one level below

A restructuring is announced. The rationale is sound, the plan is approved, and the leadership team is aligned. Six months later, the results are behind and the explanation is familiar: the organisation was not ready, people resisted, the pace was too slow.

Look closer, and the answer is usually found one level below the people who made the decision. Leading through change is shared across two levels: change is decided at the top, and it succeeds or fails one level below.

Leading through change in a climate of fatigue

Anyone leading through change is doing it in a tougher climate than a decade ago. Gartner's research, published in Harvard Business Review, found that the average employee went through 10 planned enterprise changes in 2022, up from 2 in 2016. Over the same period, the share of employees willing to support enterprise change fell from 74% to 43% (Gartner, via HBR, 2023).

Outcomes tell a similar story. In a McKinsey Global Survey on organisational redesign, only 16% of respondents said their redesign met all its objectives in the planned time (McKinsey).

These figures describe a pattern, not a verdict on any one organisation. Change is frequent, people are tired, and most programmes are not landing as planned.

Why one level below

Whatever the strategy says, employees experience change through their own manager. The manager translates the decision into daily work: what changes on Monday, what stays, who does what, and what to do when the plan meets reality. Gallup found that managers account for 70% of the variance in team engagement (Gallup, State of the American Manager). In a change, that influence does not disappear. It becomes the main channel.

The leaders in that layer carry a lot at once. They deliver today's targets while building tomorrow's structure. They explain decisions they did not make. They absorb the team's questions, and often their own doubts, with little time to prepare. The strain comes from the situation the change creates, not from the people in it.

There is also a practical problem: visibility. Without objective data, it is hard to see who is ready to lead through change, and there is rarely time to prepare every leader one by one. In my experience, senior teams often rely on impressions, and an impression of how a leader copes under pressure is hard to check.

Where the gap tends to show

Six of the 15 competencies measured by the Global Leadership Assessment (GLA360) bear directly on whether change lands. Five patterns follow from them (the first draws on two competencies), and they are the ones I look for when coaching leaders through change:

  • Keeping on with the work instead of delivering through others (Empowering People, Sharing Leadership). Under pressure, leaders take tasks back, and the team's capacity stays untouched.
  • Not growing the team's capability (Developing People). New ways of working need new skills, and nobody has time to build them.
  • Being unable to explain where the team is heading (Creating a Shared Vision). If the leader cannot say why, the team fills the silence with rumours.
  • Avoiding difficult conversations (Encouraging Constructive Dialogue). Concerns go underground and surface as quiet disengagement.
  • Managing the change instead of leading it (Leading Change). Tracking milestones is not the same as moving people.

Each of these is learnable. Each is also hard to see from inside, which is why an objective view matters.

What leaders and sponsors can do now

These steps do not need a large programme.

1. Ask each leader one question. "What are you being asked to change, and what is the hardest part of explaining it to your team?" The answers show where translation is breaking down.

2. Make the 'why' repeatable. A leader should be able to explain the reason for the change in three sentences, in their own words. If they cannot, the team will not be able to either.

3. Protect time for the conversation. Teams need a regular place to ask questions and say what worries them. A fortnightly slot is enough, but it has to happen.

4. Name the capability that will be needed. If the change means new ways of working, including the introduction of AI, say which skills the leaders themselves will need, and give them room to build them.

5. Look at the data, not the impression. Before and during the change, get a structured view of how leaders are experienced by their boss, peers and teams.

Where an objective view helps

The GLA360 is a 360-degree assessment built for business reality. It was co-developed with multinational companies, uses a global norm group of 19,000 leaders, and measures 15 observable leadership competencies. Each leader picks 5 to 15 colleagues to respond, including their boss, peers and team members, and the survey takes around 15 minutes to complete. The 45+ page report is ready the moment the survey closes.

For the leaders carrying a change, the output is practical: two Leadership Growth Areas and an action plan. Coaching then turns the plan into habits, and from that work the leader builds a personal Authentic Leadership Model, a one-page framework for how they lead, decide and communicate. This is the thinking behind GLA360 + Coaching: the assessment shows where to focus, and the coaching helps turn the plan into daily habits.
