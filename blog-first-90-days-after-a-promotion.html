The first 90 days as a new leader: where you get stuck after a promotion

You were promoted because you were very good at the work. Now your calendar is full of one-on-ones, your team looks to you for answers, and the thing you were best at (doing the work yourself) is the one thing you no longer have time for. Many new leaders feel this within weeks, and not many say it out loud.

The first 90 days as a new leader are rarely lost through weak skills. They are lost in a handful of predictable places, and knowing where they are helps you avoid them.

Why the first 90 days are so hard

A promotion changes what the job is, but nobody changes the habits that earned it. The skills that make an outstanding individual contributor are speed, personal quality and being the person who can fix anything. Those same skills can keep a new leader stuck doing instead of leading.

The situation is also built against the new leader. The calendar fills up before there is time to think. Former peers are now direct reports. Results are expected immediately, and there is little time to learn the new role while delivering. That is the pace of many promotions, not a personal failing.

It matters because of how much of a team's experience runs through its manager. Gallup's research found that managers account for 70% of the variance in team engagement (Gallup, State of the American Manager). When a leader stalls in the first months, the team feels it quickly.

Here I am speaking from experience. In my own teams and in others', I've watched what happens when an outstanding individual contributor is promoted into a leadership role and nobody prepares them for the shift. Within months, the whole team feels it: engagement drops, results stall, and sometimes up to half the team walks out. Not because the new leader lacked talent, but because no one walked them through the transition. That is the view behind the five points below.

Where new leaders get stuck

1. They keep doing the work. When a deadline is tight, the fastest option is to do the task yourself. It feels responsible. The cost is that the team never builds the capability to do it without you, and your own leadership work does not get done: setting direction, clearing obstacles, developing people.

2. They treat former peers as before. Leading people you worked beside last month is awkward. Some new leaders keep the old friendship rules and avoid hard conversations. Others overcorrect and become distant. Both leave the team unsure what has changed.

3. They leave expectations unspoken. A new leader often assumes the team knows what good looks like. The team assumes the new leader will say so. In the gap, people fill in the blanks differently.

4. They avoid giving feedback. Early feedback feels risky, because the relationship is new. But silence is also feedback. By the time a problem is big enough to raise, it has been normal for weeks.

5. They wait to be told how to lead. Many new leaders wait for someone to define the role for them. The ones who get unstuck decide, early and explicitly, how they want to lead.

What to do in the first 90 days as a new leader

These five moves are practical, and none of them needs a programme or a budget to start. Together they are a simple plan for your first 90 days as a new leader.

Name the shift. Write down what you were measured on last year and what you are measured on now. The gap between the two lists is your transition plan. Review it with your own manager in the first two weeks.

Delegate one thing that hurts. Pick a task you are good at and still own, and hand it over completely: the outcome, the deadline, and the authority to decide how. Agree when you will check in, then stay out of it until then. Expect it to be done differently, and sometimes slightly worse at first.

Have the expectations conversation. With each direct report, say what you expect, what they can expect from you, and how you will work together. Fifteen minutes, early, helps avoid months of mixed signals. With former peers, say plainly that the relationship has changed and that you want to keep what worked.

Make feedback a routine, not an event. Give small, specific feedback often, positive as well as corrective. When feedback is a regular part of a one-on-one, it stops feeling like a verdict.

Ask for an outside view. Around the three-month mark, you have enough history for others to give you useful feedback. Ask a few people you trust what you do that helps them, and what you do that gets in the way. Two or three honest answers will show you more than another month of working it out alone.

Where a structured outside view helps

Asking colleagues for feedback works, but people soften what they say to a new boss. A 360-degree assessment gives the same information in a structured, confidential form. The Global Leadership Assessment (GLA360) covers 15 observable leadership competencies and is compared against a global norm group of 19,000 leaders. Its report shows how the leader's boss, peers and team experience them, and ends with two Leadership Growth Areas and an action plan.

It works best after about three months in the role, when there is enough history for colleagues to respond to. That timing is why the First Time Leader Programme is built the way it is: the first months are for delegating, setting expectations and giving feedback, and the GLA360 comes around month three, so coaching can focus on what the data shows.

Start with one conversation

You do not have to solve the transition alone, and you do not have to wait for the organisation to hand you a plan. If you are a newly promoted leader, or you are responsible for one, a first conversation costs nothing and commits you to nothing.
